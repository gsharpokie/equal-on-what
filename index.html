What counts as inequality?

Any claim about gender inequality makes choices, often without saying so. This board makes them visible.

  • Which outcomes count. Earnings and leadership are standard measures. Life expectancy, imprisonment and deaths on the job are often left out.
  • Which direction counts. Some indexes only count gaps where women are behind.
  • Gaps or levels. A gap can stay about the same while both groups get much better off (heart disease deaths) or much worse off (overdose deaths).
  • When you look. A gap that is “stuck” over five years may have closed dramatically over forty.
  • Averages or groups. A national average for “women” or “men” can hide large differences by race, class, age and place.
  • Outcomes or treatment. A gap in outcomes doesn't by itself show unfair treatment, and equal outcomes don't prove fair treatment.

Talk about it

  1. Before you revealed anything, did you expect most knots to move to one side? Which result surprised you most?
  2. Switch the year to “Around 1980.” Which gaps closed, which reversed, and which barely moved?
  3. Open the “School & degrees” panel’s extra measures. How does the tally change? Who decides which measures belong on the board?
  4. Turn on “Weight the measures.” Which three measures would you weight most heavily, and why? Compare with a classmate.
  5. Try “Only where women are behind,” then “Only where men are behind.” Real indexes make choices like this. What does each choice assume about what inequality is?
  6. Averages for “women” and “men” hide big differences by race, class, age, and region. Pick one row and predict how it might change for a specific group.
  7. A gap tells you that groups differ, not why. For one row, list two possible explanations a sociologist would want to test.

How to read the board

Each knot’s distance from the middle shows how far apart women and men are on one measure, as a percentage: the smaller number is this much below the larger one. If women earn 81 cents for each dollar men earn, the gap is 19%. If men are four times as likely to die by homicide, the gap is 75%.

The side and color show who comes out ahead. For some measures higher is better (life expectancy, earnings). For others lower is better (homicide, poverty). A few have no obvious “better” direction, so their knots stay gray in the middle until you choose.

Why are some shares “better” and others gray? A bigger share of all degrees, or of seats in Congress, means more education or more power for that group. A share of one field is different: more women in computer science means fewer men there, and the pay effects already show up in the earnings row. Whether a field split is a problem depends on why it exists, so you decide.

Test scores are on scales without a true zero, so their rows compare the score difference to the spread of scores (the standard deviation) instead.

The index is a simple average of the signed gaps for the rows you’ve revealed. It’s a thinking tool, not a published measure.

Why this matters

Real indexes of gender equality have to choose measures, weights, and rules. The World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Index, for example, caps each score at parity, so areas where women are ahead don’t count toward the total. Other researchers have built indexes that count gaps in both directions, and critics have challenged their choices too.

Neither approach is simply right. Each one answers a different question. That is the point to take away: “Is there gender inequality?” is really several questions, and the answer depends on which ones you ask.

Other fields help here. Economists separate the raw pay gap from the gap that remains after accounting for jobs and hours. Public health researchers study why men die younger. Criminologists track who commits violence as well as who experiences it.
